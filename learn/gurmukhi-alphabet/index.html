The Gurmukhi alphabet, letter by letter

By the Ladoo team · Published August 7, 2026 · Updated August 7, 2026

Gurmukhi is the script Punjabi is written in across India and most of the diaspora. Its core is a set of 35 letters called the painti akhar (ਪੈਂਤੀ ਅੱਖਰ, “the thirty-five letters”). This page lists every character: the 35 letters, the six nukta letters, the vowel signs, the nasalization marks, the subscript letters, and the ten numerals.

Each card has a play button with the letter’s recited name (sassa, kakka, and so on), which is how the alphabet is taught aloud in Punjab. The recordings are the same audio used in the Ladoo app. Print this page and you get a clean chart without the buttons.

The 35 letters (ਪੈਂਤੀ ਅੱਖਰ)

The first three letters, ura, aira, and iri, are vowel bearers: they carry vowel sounds rather than making a consonant sound of their own. The remaining 32 are consonants, arranged in rows by where in the mouth the sound is made. Punjabi is also one of the few Indo-Aryan languages with tones, which is why some of these letters (the voiced aspirates like ਘ, ਝ, ਢ, ਧ, ਭ) sound different at the start of a word than the chart order suggests.

ੳ ਊੜਾ (ura) (vowel carrier u/o/oo)

Ura makes no sound on its own. It is a 'chair' that carries the vowels u, oo and o (ਉ ਊ ਓ).

Example: ਉਹ (uh), “that”

ਅ ਐੜਾ (aira) ə (schwa)

Aira is the only bearer with its own sound: a short 'uh'. Every consonant also hides this sound unless a vowel sign changes it.

Example: ਅੱਖ (akkh), “eye”

ੲ ਈੜੀ (iri) (vowel carrier i/ee/e)

Iri makes no sound alone. It carries the vowels i, ee and e (ਇ ਈ ਏ).

Example: ਇਹ (ih), “this”

ਸ ਸੱਸਾ (sassa) s

A clean, plain 's', the same as English.

Example: ਸੱਤ (satt), “seven”

ਹ ਹਾਹਾ (haha) h

A soft breath of 'h', as in English.

Example: ਹੱਥ (hatth), “hand”

ਕ ਕੱਕਾ (kakka) k

UNASPIRATED: no puff of air. It is the k after s in 'skip', not the breathy k in 'kite'.

Example: ਕਿਤਾਬ (kitāb), “book”

ਖ ਖੱਖਾ (khakha) kʰ

ASPIRATED: a clear puff of air follows it. Hold your hand to your mouth. You should feel the breath.

Example: ਖਾਣਾ (khāṇā), “to eat”

ਗ ਗੱਗਾ (gagga) g

A plain, hard 'g'.

Example: ਗਰਮ (garam), “hot”

ਘ ਘੱਗਾ (ghagga) gʰ

A voiced 'g' released with a puff of air: no exact English match. Say 'g' and breathe out at the same time.

Example: ਘਰ (ghar), “house”

ਙ ਙੰਙਾ (nganga) ŋ

Rare on its own; you will mostly recognise it, not write it.

Example: ਚੰਗਾ (changā), “good (m)”

ਚ ਚੱਚਾ (chacha) tʃ

Unaspirated 'ch': no extra puff.

Example: ਚਾਹ (chāh), “tea”

ਛ ਛੱਛਾ (chhachha) tʃʰ

ASPIRATED 'ch': add a breath of air after it.

Example: ਛੇ (chhe), “six”

ਜ ਜੱਜਾ (jajja) dʒ

A plain 'j'.

Example: ਜਾਣਾ (jāṇā), “to go”

ਝ ਝੱਜਾ (jhajja) dʒʰ

A voiced 'j' released with a puff of air: no exact English match.

Example: ਝਾੜੂ (jhāṛū), “broom”

ਞ ਞੰਞਾ (njanja) ɲ

Very rare; recognition only.

Example: ਪੰਜ (panj), “five”

ਟ ਟੈਂਕਾ (tainka) ʈ

RETROFLEX: curl your tongue up and back to the roof of the mouth. Deeper than the English 't'.

Example: ਛੋਟਾ (chhoṭā), “small (m)”

ਠ ਠੱਠਾ (thattha) ʈʰ

RETROFLEX + ASPIRATED: tongue curled back, plus a puff of air.

Example: ਠੀਕ (ṭhīk), “fine / okay”

ਡ ਡੱਡਾ (dadda) ɖ

RETROFLEX 'd': tongue curled up and back, deeper than the English 'd' in 'dad'.

Example: ਡਾਕਟਰ (ḍākṭar), “doctor”

ਢ ਢੱਡਾ (dhadha) ɖʰ

RETROFLEX + ASPIRATED 'd': tongue curled back, plus a puff of air.

Example: ਢਿੱਡ (ḍhiḍḍ), “stomach”

ਣ ਣਾਣਾ (nanha) ɳ

RETROFLEX 'n': tongue curled to the roof. No English equivalent; never starts a word.

Example: ਪਾਣੀ (pāṇī), “water”

ਤ ਤੱਤਾ (tatta) t̪

DENTAL: tongue tip on the back of the upper TEETH, softer than the English 't'.

Example: ਤਿੰਨ (tinn), “three”

ਥ ਥੱਥਾ (thatha) t̪ʰ

DENTAL + ASPIRATED: tongue on the teeth, plus a puff of air. Not the 'th' in 'thin'.

Example: ਥਾਂ (thāṃ), “place”

ਦ ਦੱਦਾ (dadda) d̪

DENTAL 'd': tongue on the teeth, softer than the English 'd'. English 'd' in 'dad' sits BETWEEN this and the curled-back ਡ.

Example: ਦੋ (do), “two”

ਧ ਧੱਦਾ (dhadha) d̪ʰ

DENTAL + ASPIRATED 'd': tongue on the teeth, plus a puff of air.

Example: ਧੀ (dhī), “daughter”

ਨ ਨੰਨਾ (nanna) n

A plain dental 'n', like English.

Example: ਨਾਮ (nām), “name”

ਪ ਪੱਪਾ (pappa) p

UNASPIRATED: no puff of air, like the p after s in 'spin'.

Example: ਪੰਜਾਬੀ (panjābī), “Punjabi”

ਫ ਫੱਫਾ (phappha) pʰ

ASPIRATED 'p': a clear puff of air. (In many words it now sounds like 'f'.)

Example: ਫਲ (phal), “fruit”

ਬ ਬੱਬਾ (babba) b

A plain 'b'.

Example: ਬੂਹਾ (būhā), “door”

ਭ ਭੱਬਾ (bhabha) bʰ

A voiced 'b' released with a puff of air: no exact English match.

Example: ਭਰਾ (bharā), “brother”

ਮ ਮੱਮਾ (mamma) m

A plain 'm', like English.

Example: ਮਾਤਾ (mātā), “mother”

ਯ ਯੱਯਾ (yaiya) j

A plain 'y' glide.

Example: ਯਾਦ (yād), “memory / remembered”

ਰ ਰਾਰਾ (rara) ɾ

TAPPED: flick the tongue once against the ridge. Do NOT bunch the tongue like the English 'r'.

Example: ਰੋਟੀ (roṭī), “bread”

ਲ ਲੱਲਾ (lalla) l

A plain 'l', like English.

Example: ਲਾਲ (lāl), “red”

ਵ ਵਾਵਾ (vava) ʋ

A soft sound halfway between 'v' and 'w': lips loose, not biting the lip.

Example: ਵੱਡਾ (vaḍḍā), “big (m)”

ੜ ੜਾੜਾ (rarra) ɽ

RETROFLEX flap: curl the tongue back and flick it forward. No English match; never starts a word.

Example: ਕੁੜੀ (kuṛī), “girl”

The six nukta letters

A dot (nukta, ਼) under a letter marks sounds that entered Punjabi through Persian, Arabic, and English loanwords, like the z in zamin or the f in fauj. In everyday speech many Punjabi speakers merge these with the base letters, so you will hear both pronunciations.

ਸ਼ ਸ਼ੱਸ਼ਾ (shha) ʃ

Sassa (ਸ) with a dot below becomes 'sh'.

Example: ਸ਼ਾਮ (shām), “evening”

ਖ਼ ਖ਼ੱਖ਼ਾ (khha) x

Khakha (ਖ) with a dot below: a raspy 'kh' from the throat.

Example: ਖ਼ੁਸ਼ (khush), “happy”

ਗ਼ ਗ਼ੱਗ਼ਾ (ghha) ɣ

Gagga (ਗ) with a dot below: a soft throaty 'gh'.

Example: ਗ਼ਲਤ (galat), “wrong”

ਜ਼ ਜ਼ੱਜ਼ਾ (za) z

Jajja (ਜ) with a dot below becomes 'z'.

Example: ਬਜ਼ਾਰ (bazār), “market / bazaar”

ਫ਼ ਫ਼ੱਫ਼ਾ (fa) f

Phappha (ਫ) with a dot below becomes a true 'f'.

Example: ਫ਼ੋਨ (fon), “phone”

ਲ਼ ਲ਼ੱਲ਼ਾ (lla) ɭ

Lalla (ਲ) with a dot below: a retroflex 'l'. Regional; recognition only.

Example: ਵਾਲ਼ (vāl), “hair”

The vowel signs (ਲਗਾਂ ਮਾਤਰਾਂ)

Punjabi has ten vowel sounds but only nine written vowel signs; the tenth, mukta, is the short “a” a consonant carries by default with no mark at all. Each sign attaches to a consonant before, after, above, or below it.

ਾ ਕੰਨਾ (kanna) aː

A vertical line AFTER a letter that lengthens its vowel to a long 'aa'. With Aira it forms ਆ.

Example: ਕਾਲਾ (kālā), “black”

ਿ ਸਿਹਾਰੀ (sihari) ɪ

WRITTEN BEFORE the letter but READ AFTER it, a common surprise. With Iri it forms ਇ.

Example: ਪਿੰਡ (piṇḍ), “village”

ੀ ਬਿਹਾਰੀ (bihari) iː

Written after the letter, it gives a long 'ee'. With Iri it forms ਈ.

Example: ਕੀ (kī), “what”

ੁ ਔਂਕੜ (aunkar) ʊ

A small mark UNDER the letter for a short 'u'. With Ura it forms ਉ.

Example: ਮੁੰਡਾ (muṇḍā), “boy”

ੂ ਦੁਲੈਂਕੜ (dulainkar) uː

A mark under the letter for a long 'oo'. With Ura it forms ਊ.

Example: ਸਕੂਲ (skūl), “school”

ੇ ਲਾਂਵ (lavan) eː

A stroke above the letter for a long 'e'. With Iri it forms ਏ.

Example: ਮੇਰਾ (merā), “my”

ੈ ਦੁਲਾਂਵ (dulavan) ɛː

Two strokes above for an open 'ai'. With Aira it forms ਐ.

Example: ਪੈਸਾ (paisā), “money”

ੋ ਹੋੜਾ (hora) oː

A mark above the letter for a long 'o'. With Ura it forms ਓ.

Example: ਦੋਸਤ (dost), “friend”

ੌ ਕਨੌੜਾ (kanaura) ɔː

Two marks above for an open 'au'. With Aira it forms ਔ.

Example: ਮੌਸਮ (mausam), “weather”

Nasalization and doubling

Tippi and bindi nasalize a vowel (like the n in the French bon); which one is written depends on the vowel it sits with. Addak doubles the consonant that follows it, a length difference that changes meaning.

ੰ ਟਿੱਪੀ (tippi) ◌̃ (nasal)

A small mark that nasalizes the vowel by sending air through the nose. Used over short/most vowels.

Example: ਤੂੰ (tūṃ), “you (informal)”

ਂ ਬਿੰਦੀ (bindi) ◌̃ (nasal)

A dot that nasalizes the vowel; used over the open vowels (kanna, lavan, etc.).

Example: ਹਾਂ (hāṃ), “am / yes”

ੱ ਅੱਧਕ (addak) ː (doubling)

Doubles the NEXT consonant. Hold it a beat longer. ਪੱਕਾ is 'pakkaa', not 'pakaa'.

Example: ਇੱਕ (ikk), “one”

The subscript letters (ਪੈਰੀਂ)

Three letters can be written “in the foot” (pairin) of another letter, tucked underneath to form a cluster, like the r in the word Punjabi itself (ਪੰਜਾਬੀ uses none, but ਪ੍ਰੇਮ, prem, does).

੍ਰ ਪੈਰੀਂ ਰਾਰਾ (pairin rara) r (blended under a letter)

A small copy of Rara tucked under another letter. The two sounds blend into one quick cluster, so ਪ + ੍ਰ reads 'pr'.

Example: ਪ੍ਰੇਮ (prem), “love”

੍ਹ ਪੈਰੀਂ ਹਾਹਾ (pairin haha) h (breath under a letter, often tone)

A small Haha hiding under a letter. It adds a light breath that in everyday speech usually turns into a tone, as in ਪੜ੍ਹ.

Example: ਪੜ੍ਹ (parh), “read / study”

੍ਵ ਪੈਰੀਂ ਵਾਵਾ (pairin vava) v/w (blended under a letter)

A rare little Vava under a letter, adding a quick w glide. You will mostly meet it in careful or classical spellings like ਸ੍ਵਰ.

Example: ਸ੍ਵਰ (svar), “vowel sound”

The numerals

Gurmukhi has its own digits, used in the Guru Granth Sahib and traditional texts. Modern Punjabi writing mostly uses Western digits, but you will meet these in religious and older material.

੦ ਸਿਫ਼ਰ (sifar) 0

Zero. Say 'sifar'.

Example: ਸਿਫ਼ਰ (sifar), “zero”

੧ ਇੱਕ (ikk) 1

One. Say 'ikk'.

Example: ਇੱਕ (ikk), “one”

੨ ਦੋ (do) 2

Two. Say 'do'.

Example: ਦੋ (do), “two”

੩ ਤਿੰਨ (tinn) 3

Three. Say 'tinn'.

Example: ਤਿੰਨ (tinn), “three”

੪ ਚਾਰ (chaar) 4

Four. Say 'chaar'.

Example: ਚਾਰ (chaar), “four”

੫ ਪੰਜ (panj) 5

Five. Say 'panj'.

Example: ਪੰਜ (panj), “five”

੬ ਛੇ (chhe) 6

Six. Say 'chhe'.

Example: ਛੇ (chhe), “six”

੭ ਸੱਤ (satt) 7

Seven. Say 'satt'.

Example: ਸੱਤ (satt), “seven”

੮ ਅੱਠ (atth) 8

Eight. Say 'atth'.

Example: ਅੱਠ (atth), “eight”

੯ ਨੌਂ (naun) 9

Nine. Say 'nau(n)', through the nose.

Example: ਨੌਂ (naun), “nine”
