Punjabi pronunciation: tones, aspiration, and retroflex

By the Ladoo team · Published August 31, 2026

Punjabi is one of the few tonal languages in the Indo-Aryan family, and that single fact explains most of what romanized Punjabi gets wrong. Three sound systems need your attention: the tones, the aspirated consonants, and the retroflex ones. Every example below has native audio, and if you grew up hearing Punjabi, your ear already knows all of this; the work is teaching your mouth.

The tones: why ਘਰ does not sound like "ghar"

Punjabi has three tones: level, low, and high. Historically breathy letters like ਘ ਝ ਢ ਭ lost their breathiness and left tone behind, so at the start of a word ਘਰ (house) is pronounced like "kar" said with a low dip, not "g-har". This is why a romanization can be spelled faithfully and still mislead you. Listen to the pair:

ਕਰ kar do

Plain k, level tone. The baseline.

ਘਰ ghar house

Spelled with ghagga, but listen: it starts like k with a low, dipping tone. The gh letters carry tone, not breathy voice.

ਪੜ੍ਹ paṛh read (command)

The buried haha under the letter surfaces as tone, not as an audible h.

ਢੋਲ ḍhol drum (dhol)

Same story with dhadda: closer to 'tol' with a low tone than to English 'dhol'.

ਝੂਠ jhūṭh lie (untruth)

And with jhajja: a ch-like start plus tone.

Aspiration: the puff you must learn to control

ਕ and ਖ are the same consonant with and without a burst of air, and the pairs run through the whole alphabet: ਤ/ਥ, ਪ/ਫ, ਚ/ਛ. English aspirates automatically at the start of words ("pin") and drops it after s ("spin"), so the skill for an English speaker is control, producing the puff on demand and holding it back on demand.

ਕਰ kar do

No puff of air on the k.

ਖਾਓ khāo eat (polite command)

Same spot in the mouth, now with a burst of air. Hold your palm in front of your lips and feel the difference.

ਥਾਂ thāṃ place

Aspirated dental th. Not the English 'th' of 'think'; a t with air.

ਫਲ phal fruit

Aspirated p, romanized ph. It is not an f, even though it looks like one on paper.

Retroflex vs dental: two t's, two d's

Punjabi splits t and d into two families English does not have: dental (tongue on the teeth: ਤ ਦ ਨ) and retroflex (tongue curled back: ਟ ਡ ਣ). The English t and d sit in between, which is why both Punjabi sets sound identical to a beginner and unmistakably different a month later. The retroflex flap ੜ is its own beast: one strike of the curled tongue on its way down.

ਦਿਲ dil heart

Dental d: tongue tip on the teeth.

ਡਰ ḍar fear

Retroflex d: tongue tip curled back to the roof of the mouth. Darker, rounder sound.

ਹੱਥ hatth hand

Dental th at the end, on the teeth.

ਰੋਟੀ roṭī roti / bread

The t in roti is retroflex. Say it on the teeth and it stops sounding Punjabi.

ਸੜਕ saṛak road

The flap: the tongue curls back and strikes once on the way down. Neither r nor d.

ਪੜ੍ਹਨਾ paṛhnā to read / study

Flap plus tone in one word. When this one sounds right, you are most of the way there.

How to practice, in order

Start with retroflex vs dental, because it carries the most everyday words and the contrast is learnable in a week of listening. Then aspiration, with the palm test. Leave the tones for last and learn them the way native speakers did, by ear and repetition rather than by rule; drilling words like ghar and parhna with audio does more than any chart. Common questions

Is Punjabi a tonal language?

Yes. Punjabi has three tones (level, low, and high), which is rare among Indo-Aryan languages. The tones mostly replaced the old breathy-voiced consonants, so spelling still shows ਘ ਝ ਢ ਭ where speech now shows tone.

Can adults learn the tones?

Yes, by listening rather than memorizing rules. Heritage speakers usually already hear the tones without knowing they do; the gap is producing them, which speaking practice with feedback closes.

Why does romanized Punjabi keep misleading me?

Because Latin letters cannot carry tone, aspiration, and the retroflex contrast at once. Gurmukhi marks all three, which is a practical reason to learn the script early: what is written is what is said.
