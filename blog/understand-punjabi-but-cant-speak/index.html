You Understand Punjabi but Can't Speak It. Here Is How to Fix That

By the Ladoo team · Published August 14, 2026 · Updated August 14, 2026

You follow every word at the dinner table. You laugh at the jokes before anyone translates them. Then someone turns to you and asks a question in Punjabi, and your answer comes out in English, again. If that is you, you are not bad at Punjabi. You are what linguists call a receptive bilingual, and you are far closer to speaking than you think.

It has a name, and it is extremely common

Receptive bilingualism means you can understand a language well but cannot produce it. It is one of the most common outcomes for children of immigrants everywhere in the world, and it is the default outcome for Punjabi in the diaspora: parents and grandparents spoke Punjabi to you, you answered in English, and everyone understood each other, so the arrangement stuck. Nobody failed. The system just never asked you to speak.

That distinction matters, because the fix for "never practiced speaking" is completely different from the fix for "never learned the language." You do not need to start from zero. You need to convert knowledge you already have.

Why understanding does not turn into speaking on its own

Listening and speaking are different skills built by different practice. Understanding runs on recognition: the word arrives, and your brain matches it against thousands of hours of dinner-table audio. Speaking runs on recall: you must find the word yourself, assemble the grammar, and move your mouth through sounds English never taught you, all in real time while someone waits.

Recognition improves every time you overhear a conversation. Recall improves only when you actually produce the language, and if every conversation lets you answer in English, you can live inside Punjabi for thirty years and never build it. That is the whole trap. It is also the reason the trap is escapable: the input half, the half that takes thousands of hours, is already done.

Start embarrassingly small

The biggest mistake receptive bilinguals make is trying to jump straight to full sentences in front of the people they most want to impress. Then one shaky sentence gets a chuckle from an auntie and the experiment ends for another year. Do the opposite: shrink the task until failure is impossible.

Pick five formulaic replies you hear constantly. Answers to "how are you," "have you eaten," "when did you get here." Not vocabulary lists: real replies to real questions you already know are coming. Use only those for two weeks, and keep answering everything else in English without guilt. You are not trying to hold a conversation yet. You are teaching your mouth that producing Punjabi is a thing it does.

Practice where nobody is watching

The freeze is not a language problem, it is a stage-fright problem, so remove the stage. Three ways that work:

  • Shadowing. Play a short piece of Punjabi audio and repeat it out loud a beat behind the speaker, copying the rhythm and tone. You are borrowing someone else's sentences instead of building your own, which is exactly the right difficulty when recall is the weak skill.
  • Narrate to yourself. While cooking or driving, say what you are doing in Punjabi. It feels ridiculous and it works, because it forces recall with zero audience.
  • Talk to something that cannot judge you. Voice notes to yourself count. So does an AI speaking partner: Ladoo's tutor, Bhaji, listens to your spoken Punjabi and answers back, and it has infinite patience for the sentence you needed four tries to finish. No auntie has that feature.

Learn to read, even though your goal is speaking

This one surprises people. If all of your Punjabi lives in your ear, every word you know is trapped in other people's voices, and you can only practice what you happen to overhear. Reading breaks that dependency. Gurmukhi is a phonetic script with 35 core letters: what you see is what you say, which makes it one of the easier scripts anywhere to pick up. Most learners can decode simple words within a few weeks. Start with our free Gurmukhi alphabet chart, which covers every letter and vowel sign with audio.

Once you can read, you can pause, look at a word, and practice it slowly, which is impossible with speech. Reading also fixes the words you have been mishearing since childhood. Nearly every receptive bilingual discovers that a word they have heard a thousand times is not quite what they thought it was.

Renegotiate the family arrangement

Eventually you have to bring it back to the people you learned it from, because they are the reason you want this. Tell one person, ideally the most patient one, that you are learning to speak and you want them to let you answer in Punjabi, wait while you find words, and not switch to English to rescue you. Grandparents are usually the best partners: they have the time, they are the least likely to tease, and nothing you will ever do delights them more.

Keep the sessions short and regular. Ten minutes of real production beats an hour of nodding along.

Expect the awkward phase, and know that it ends

There is a stretch, usually a few months, where you sound worse than you feel you should, because your ear is decades ahead of your mouth and you can hear every one of your own mistakes. That gap is not evidence you are failing. It is the defining feature of being a receptive bilingual, and it closes from the speaking side only. Every wobbly sentence you push out narrows it. The people who get through are not the talented ones; they are the ones who kept talking badly long enough to talk well.
