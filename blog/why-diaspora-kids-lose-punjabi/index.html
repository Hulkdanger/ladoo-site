Why Diaspora Kids Lose Punjabi

By the Ladoo team · Published August 14, 2026 · Updated August 14, 2026

Punjabi is not a small language. It has well over 100 million speakers worldwide, and it is nowhere near endangered globally. And yet in Punjabi families in Canada, the UK, the US, and Australia, the same quiet thing keeps happening: the grandchildren cannot speak it. This is not carelessness, and it is not any one family's failure. It is one of the most predictable patterns in linguistics, and understanding it is the first step to interrupting it.

The three-generation rule

Sociolinguists have documented the same arc in immigrant communities all over the world, so consistently that it has a name: three-generation language shift. The first generation arrives fluent in Punjabi and learns as much English as life requires. Their children grow up bilingual but lopsided: Punjabi at home, English everywhere else, and English gradually wins because school, friends, television, and eventually their own inner voice all run on it. By the third generation, the home language is a handful of food words and phrases used with grandparents. Three generations, start to finish, unless something interrupts the slide.

The sobering part is that no one in this story ever decides to give up Punjabi. Every step is a reasonable choice made by people doing their best, which is exactly why the pattern is so hard to see while it is happening to you.

The headline numbers hide it

On paper, diaspora Punjabi looks like it is booming. In Canada's 2021 census, Punjabi was the second most common mother tongue in the country after English and French, at about 667,000 people. In England and Wales, the 2021 census counted about 291,000 people with Punjabi as their main language, up from 273,000 a decade earlier.

But look closer at the Canadian numbers and the shift is right there: of the roughly 667,000 people whose mother tongue is Punjabi, only about 520,000 speak it predominantly at home. That gap, well over a hundred thousand people, is largely made of homes where the language of childhood is no longer the language of daily life. And the totals keep rising mainly because new immigration keeps replenishing the first generation, not because transmission to the next one improved. Immigration masks attrition. If arrivals slowed, the census lines would bend down within a generation, the way they have for many older immigrant languages.

How the gap actually forms

The mechanics are ordinary and familiar:

  • School flips the balance. Until age four or five, a diaspora child's Punjabi is often ahead of their English. Then school delivers eight hours a day of English immersion, English-speaking friends, and the strong signal that English is the language of achievement. No home routine competes with that volume.
  • Kids answer in English, and it works. Parents ask in Punjabi, children reply in English, everyone understands, dinner continues. Each exchange is harmless; years of them quietly remove every occasion the child had to actually produce Punjabi.
  • Parents accommodate. Busy parents want to be understood, and many were themselves told that English mattered more. So they switch, often without noticing, especially once homework and teenagers enter the picture.
  • The script never arrives. Many diaspora kids never learn to read Gurmukhi, so Punjabi exists for them only as sound. A language you cannot read is hard to study, hard to text in, and impossible to keep up with on your own.

What survives is understanding

Here is the twist: the language rarely disappears entirely. Because comprehension is built by exposure and speech is built by practice, thousands of hours of overheard Punjabi leave most diaspora kids able to follow conversations they cannot join. Linguists call this receptive bilingualism, and it is the signature condition of the second and third generation. It feels like loss, but it is really an enormous, half-finished asset: the hardest part of the language is already in your head. What it actually costs

The loss is not abstract. It is being at your own grandmother's kitchen table with a translator. It is catching the emotion in an argument but not the content, sitting at the gurdwara understanding the melody and not the words, hearing your own name pronounced correctly only by relatives. Language carries the jokes, the blessings, the scolding, and the songs that do not translate, and when it goes, those go with it. Most people feel this sharply exactly once: when there is suddenly less time left with the people they wanted to talk to.

The pattern is reversible

None of this is destiny. Language shift is a default, not a law, and the interruptions that work are known:

  • Keep Punjabi answerable. The single highest-leverage habit in a household is not "speak Punjabi to the kids." It is "let kids answer in Punjabi and wait for it." Production, not exposure, is what the default erodes first.
  • Teach the script early. Gurmukhi is phonetic and learnable in weeks, and it turns Punjabi from a sound in the air into something a person can study, text, and read on their own. Our free Gurmukhi alphabet chart with audio is a good starting point at any age.
  • Give it a life outside the kitchen. Music, films, stories, gurdwara classes, cousins on video calls. A language survives in a family when it has more than one room to live in.
  • Start from where you actually are. Second and third generation learners are not beginners; they are receptive bilinguals with decades of listening behind them. Tools built for that starting point, with real speaking practice, move much faster than generic beginner courses.

The three-generation clock is real, but it only runs while nobody is looking at it. Plenty of families, and plenty of adults who thought their Punjabi was gone, have turned it around. The language your grandparents carried across an ocean is a lot harder to kill than a census line suggests. It is waiting, mostly intact, in the part of you that still laughs before the translation.
